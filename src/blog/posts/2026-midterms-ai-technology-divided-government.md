The 2026 midterms have produced a significant shift in Washington. On November 3, voters chose every member of the House and roughly one-third of the Senate. Early reporting on November 4 indicates that Democrats have won majorities in both chambers, leaving President Donald Trump to face a Congress controlled by the opposition for the final two years of his term. Results are still being counted and certified, so seat totals may move; the direction of control is the immediate political fact to watch. [Associated Press election coverage](https://apnews.com/article/9c5c4c5283f58b1ae0786e2f410c21a0)

That shift matters for technology because Congress shapes statutes, budgets, investigations, and confirmations. It does not mean a new federal AI regime appears overnight. The president remains in office, can veto legislation, and retains substantial authority over executive agencies, foreign policy, and many trade and national-security decisions. Democrats would need Republican votes to override a veto, and they will have to agree among themselves on what technology policy should do.

## The immediate change is oversight

The incoming Congress can use hearings, subpoenas, investigations, and budget negotiations to scrutinize how federal agencies use AI and how the administration carries out its technology agenda. That could put algorithmic decision-making, procurement, data protection, export controls, federal research priorities, and the implementation of executive orders under closer examination.

Oversight can reveal contracts, implementation choices, and agency practices even when lawmakers cannot pass a new law. Hearings also give technology firms, civil-rights groups, researchers, and workers a public forum to make their cases. The result may be more transparency and political pressure well before any binding AI legislation gets through both chambers.

## AI rules: scrutiny may rise before the law changes

The United States still lacks one comprehensive federal statute that regulates AI across the private sector. The Congressional Research Service has described federal activity as a mix of targeted provisions, agency programs, and executive-branch priorities. That leaves important questions—such as disclosure, liability, safety testing, and the division of authority between federal and state governments—open to further debate. [CRS overview of AI policy](https://crsreports.congress.gov/product/pdf/R/R47644)

A Democratic majority could revive proposals on consumer protection, deepfakes, discrimination, data privacy, automated decisions, and transparency. It could also press agencies to explain how their existing authorities apply to AI systems. But “could” is doing real work here: the party has no guarantee of consensus on a single bill, and a measure opposed by the president faces a veto. Incremental legislation, investigations, appropriations language, and bipartisan provisions are more plausible near-term routes than a sweeping statute.

## Data centers put AI policy on the ground

The physical footprint of AI is becoming a local and national political issue. Data centers require land, power, water, transmission upgrades, and large capital investments. The administration’s 2025 actions call for faster federal permitting of qualifying data-center infrastructure and promote exports of an American AI technology stack. A new Congress can examine those programs, the public costs attached to them, and the way federal support is allocated. [Data-center infrastructure order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/07/accelerating-federal-permitting-of-data-center-infrastructure/), [AI export order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/07/promoting-the-export-of-the-american-ai-technology-stack/)

That does not put every data-center decision in Congress’s hands. Local zoning, state utility commissions, private power contracts, and federal permitting agencies all shape where facilities get built and who pays for grid upgrades. The political pressure is likely to center on accountability: whether projects deliver promised jobs, whether electricity costs shift to households, and whether communities have a meaningful voice.

## Chips, exports, and competition with China

Semiconductor supply chains and export controls sit at the intersection of technology, trade, and national security. The administration’s AI Action Plan emphasizes U.S. leadership, infrastructure, and exporting American AI systems. A Congress led by the opposition can challenge the details and costs of that approach, request intelligence and agency testimony, and shape funding for research, manufacturing, and enforcement.

Yet export rules are often implemented through executive agencies, and foreign-policy powers remain with the president. Expect political scrutiny and uncertainty around the boundaries of controls, allied coordination, and access to advanced chips. Companies making long-term investments should track agency actions and enacted appropriations, rather than treating a congressional majority as an immediate reversal of current policy.

## Cybersecurity and election integrity

The campaign cycle has again highlighted the risks of synthetic media, impersonation, influence operations, and attacks on election infrastructure. A new Congress can revisit support for state and local election offices, federal incident response, public reporting, and rules governing political content generated or altered by AI. It can also ask platforms and agencies how they detect and communicate about coordinated manipulation.

The practical challenge is designing safeguards that protect voters without giving government a vague power to police political speech. Technical provenance labels can help establish where a file came from, but they do not prove that its claims are true; detection systems can miss altered media or flag legitimate content. Resilience still depends on multiple layers: trusted local election administration, rapid public correction, secure systems, and clear lines of responsibility.

## What businesses and builders should watch

For the technology sector, the election changes the odds and the tone of policymaking more than it changes the rules today. Companies should watch committee leadership, hearing agendas, agency oversight requests, federal budget negotiations, and any bipartisan bills that could move despite divided government. Data-center developers should follow state and local permitting and utility proceedings alongside federal policy. AI product teams should keep records of training data, evaluations, human review, and incident response; those practices are useful regardless of which proposal eventually becomes law.

For the public, the key questions are concrete: Who pays for AI infrastructure? Which decisions can be challenged when an automated system gets something wrong? How are children, workers, and consumers protected? How should AI-generated political media be labeled? A new Congress can bring these questions into sharper focus, but the answers will depend on legislation, agency implementation, courts, states, and the choices technology companies make.

The 2026 midterms therefore mark a change in leverage, not a settled technology agenda. A Democratic Congress can investigate, negotiate, and block or reshape parts of the president’s program. Turning campaign themes into durable AI and technology policy will require agreement across a divided government—and rules that can keep pace with fast-moving systems without losing sight of the people affected by them.
