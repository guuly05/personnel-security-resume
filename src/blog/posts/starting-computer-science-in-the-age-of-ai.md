If you are starting a computer science degree this semester, you may already have heard the same prediction everywhere: *AI has ended computer science.* It can make the future sound like a race you have already lost before your first lecture.

Do not let that noise define your education. AI is a powerful tool, but using a tool well still requires knowledge, judgment, and a clear understanding of the problem. The field is changing quickly; the reasons to learn computer science have not disappeared.

## Think of AI the way engineers think of CAD

Before computer-aided design software, engineers drew plans and models by hand. That work took time and care. CAD tools made it much faster to create, revise, and inspect designs. They changed the workflow, but they did not remove the need for engineers. Someone still has to understand the requirements, choose a sound design, check whether it works, and take responsibility for the result.

AI is a similar kind of shift for software. It can help draft code, explain an unfamiliar concept, suggest tests, or offer a starting point when you are stuck. But a suggestion is not the same thing as a correct, secure, maintainable solution. Someone still needs to decide what should be built, whether the output is right, how it fits into a larger system, and what to do when it fails.

That is where computer scientists come in. The better these tools become, the more useful it is to understand what they are doing and where their limits are. Learn to work with AI, but do not let it do all your thinking for you. Ask it to explain an idea, then check that explanation. Use it to explore an approach, then try implementing or changing the solution yourself. Your goal is to become the person who can guide and evaluate the tool.

## Make room for a “seventh course”

Your university courses give you structure, deadlines, and a foundation. They cannot cover every tool and skill you will meet outside the classroom. If you take six courses in a semester, consider making a seventh course for yourself: a small, consistent learning track that you choose and manage. It is a way to get ahead of the syllabus and build skills before you need them in an advanced class, a project, or an internship.

That does not have to mean enrolling in another class or filling every spare hour. It might mean setting aside a few hours each week to learn Git, build a small program, study how the internet works, or follow one topic until it makes sense. Keep the scope realistic so that your own course supports your degree instead of pushing you toward burnout. Consistency over a semester will take you further than an ambitious plan you cannot sustain.

Choose a goal you can demonstrate. Make a command-line tool, put a simple website online, write a program that reads and transforms a file, or contribute a small fix to a project. When something breaks, investigate it before immediately asking AI for the answer. Keep notes on what confused you and what helped. A finished, understandable project teaches you more than a long list of tutorials you only watched.

## Build the fundamentals that transfer

Start with the habits and ideas that will help in every class and project:

- **Learn Git early.** Practice saving changes, reading a diff, making branches, and recovering from mistakes. Version control is a normal part of working on software with other people, and it gives you a safe history of your own work.
- **Understand programming, not just syntax.** Get comfortable breaking a problem into steps, using variables and control flow, organizing data, writing functions, and debugging one assumption at a time. Then study data structures and algorithms as ways to make those solutions clearer and more efficient.
- **Look below the surface with C or C++.** Working with a lower-level language can help you see memory, types, compilation, and the relationship between a program and the machine more directly. You do not need to use these languages for everything; learning what they expose can strengthen your understanding of higher-level tools.
- **Learn how the internet works.** Build a basic picture of clients and servers, DNS, IP addresses, TCP, HTTP, and TLS. You will understand web applications much better when you can follow what happens between typing a URL and receiving a response.
- **Practice explaining your work.** If you can describe what your program does, why you chose an approach, and how you know it works, you are developing more than the ability to produce code.

These topics connect. Git helps you manage changes while you build a networked application. Programming fundamentals help you understand the server logic. Networking explains how the browser reaches it. The more of the chain you can see, the less mysterious software becomes.

## Get deeply comfortable with one language

You will probably meet several programming languages during your degree. It can feel as if you have to master all of them at once. Instead, choose one language that fits your current learning and become comfortable solving a range of problems with it. Learn to read errors, use functions and data structures, organize a project, test behavior, and debug code you did not write yourself.

Once you understand those ideas in one language, a new language is easier to approach. You still have to learn its syntax, libraries, conventions, and distinctive features; languages are not all interchangeable. But many core ideas—conditions, loops, functions, data, abstraction, and algorithms—carry across. That means you can spend less time feeling lost in unfamiliar punctuation and more time understanding how the new language expresses a problem you already know how to solve.

Depth in one language gives you a home base. Breadth can come later, as your courses and projects give you a reason to explore.

## Learn for understanding, not for comparison

It is natural to want to be ahead of your classmates, but comparison is a poor measure of progress. One student may have coded for years; another may be seeing a terminal for the first time. Focus on whether you understand more this month than you did last month, whether you can solve a problem with less help, and whether you can explain your choices clearly.

Use your lectures, but do not make them the boundary of your curiosity. Ask why a concept works. Try a small experiment. Read documentation. Build something imperfect and improve it. Ask for help when you need it, including from classmates, instructors, books, and AI tools—but keep yourself involved in the reasoning.

Computer science is not over because a tool can generate code. Tools have always changed how people work. Your opportunity is to learn the ideas underneath them well enough to use them thoughtfully, check their work, and create things that solve real problems. Start with curiosity, build your fundamentals one week at a time, and give yourself permission to learn this field at a human pace.
