Computer science did not begin when someone switched on a computer. It grew out of much older questions: What counts as a calculation? Which problems can be solved by a precise recipe? How can a machine remember a step, repeat it, and choose what to do next? How might distant machines share messages? And could a machine learn patterns rather than follow only hand-written rules?

The history that follows is a long story of people turning those questions into mathematics, mechanisms, circuits, programs, networks, and learning systems. It gives special attention to Alan Turing, as requested, but no one person invented computer science. A genuinely complete list of every contributor would fill libraries; even the familiar milestones were made by teams, technicians, programmers, operators, teachers, funders, and users whose names are often missing from the surviving record. This is a detailed route through the best-known foundations, with some of those less visible contributions brought back into view.

## 1. Before the computer: the idea of a procedure

Long before electricity, people used counting boards, abaci, tables, and written procedures to make repeated calculations less error-prone. The word *algorithm* is linked to the Latinized name of the ninth-century Persian mathematician Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī, whose books helped transmit systematic methods for arithmetic and algebra. A machine is not required for an algorithm: at its most basic, an algorithm is a finite, exact procedure for turning an input into an answer.

In the seventeenth century, Blaise Pascal built a gear-driven adding machine, while Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz designed a stepped calculator and argued that calculation could be expressed in binary digits, 0 and 1. Leibniz did not build an electronic binary computer, but the combination of symbolic representation and mechanical rules became a powerful idea. The Jacquard loom, introduced in 1801, used punched cards to control which threads were raised in a weave. The cards did not calculate, but they made a striking demonstration: a machine could be made to follow a changeable sequence of instructions.

Charles Babbage took that possibility into arithmetic. His Difference Engine was designed to generate mathematical tables using repeated addition rather than error-prone hand calculation. In the 1830s he developed the far more ambitious Analytical Engine: it was to have a “Store” for numbers, a “Mill” for arithmetic, punched-card input, and a way to repeat or branch through operations. Those are recognizable ancestors of memory, a processor, input, and control flow. The complete engine was never built in Babbage’s lifetime. Its significance lies in the design and in the idea that a machine with the right components could be general-purpose, not in a working Victorian computer hidden in a museum.

![An original interpretation of the Analytical Engine plan, with its Store, Mill, punched-card input, and output path; this is a diagram, not a photograph of a built machine.](/images/computer-science-history/analytical-engine.svg)

![Charles Babbage in an 1871 obituary portrait, reproduced from the Illustrated London News.](/images/computer-science-history/charles-babbage.jpg)

Image: public-domain portrait reproduced by [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Charles_Babbage_1860.jpg).

Ada Lovelace, a mathematician who studied Babbage’s plans closely, translated an 1842 description of the Engine by Italian engineer Luigi Federico Menabrea and added extensive notes in 1843. Her Note G describes a sequence of operations for calculating Bernoulli numbers. Because it was written for a machine that did not yet exist, the sequence is often called the first published computer program. The label is useful if it is not allowed to erase the collaboration: Babbage devised and revised the machine, Menabrea explained it, and Lovelace developed its mathematical and conceptual possibilities. She also saw that a symbolic machine might manipulate things other than numbers if those things could be represented according to rules.

![Watercolour portrait of Ada Lovelace by Alfred Edward Chalon, painted around 1840.](/images/computer-science-history/ada-lovelace.jpg)

Image: Alfred Edward Chalon; public-domain artwork, digitized by the Science Museum Group and shared through [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ada_Lovelace_portrait.jpg).

The nineteenth century supplied another essential piece. George Boole developed an algebra in which statements could be combined and evaluated as true or false. In the 1930s, Claude Shannon showed how Boolean logic could describe electrical relay circuits: a logical AND, OR, or NOT could be implemented as a pattern of open and closed switches. This is the bridge from abstract logic to the switching circuits inside digital hardware. Nor was that the only route into logic. Logicians Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell formalized systems of reasoning; David Hilbert posed foundational problems; Kurt Gödel showed that formal mathematics has limits. Computer science would inherit both the ambition to mechanize reasoning and proof that no machine can settle every well-formed question.

## 2. Alan Turing and the machine that defined a boundary

In 1936, while still a young mathematician, Alan Turing published “On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem.” The decision problem asked whether there could be a mechanical procedure that would decide, for every statement in a formal mathematical system, whether it was provable. Turing attacked the question by first making “mechanical procedure” precise.

His model is deliberately spare. Imagine a long paper tape divided into cells, each holding a symbol. A head can read the current cell, write a symbol, move one cell left or right, and change to one of a finite number of internal states. A compact table says what to do for each combination of state and symbol. A person doing arithmetic with paper and pencil, Turing reasoned, could be described as carrying out just such elementary steps. A machine could therefore be defined by a finite instruction table rather than by a picture of gears or motors.

![A simplified Turing machine: a finite-state head reads and writes symbols on a tape, following a transition rule.](/images/computer-science-history/turing-machine.svg)

The tape model is an explanation of computation, not a proposed 1930s product design. Its power came from showing that a universal machine could read the coded description of another machine and simulate its steps. In modern language: one general mechanism could execute many programs because the instructions themselves could be represented as data. Turing’s reasoning also established limits. There are precise questions about computations that no algorithm can answer for all possible inputs. More hardware cannot solve a problem that is mathematically undecidable.

Turing’s result appeared alongside a related one by Alonzo Church, whose lambda calculus offered a different formal account of effective computation. The equivalence of these approaches became known as the Church–Turing thesis: every function that can be computed by an effective procedure can be computed by a Turing machine. It is a thesis about what “effective computation” means, not a theorem saying that every physical computer must literally contain an infinite paper tape.

![Alan Turing at Princeton University in 1936.](/images/computer-science-history/alan-turing.jpg)

Image: anonymous 1936 photograph, public domain in the United States; [Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Alan_Turing_(1912-1954)_at_Princeton_University_in_1936.jpg).

Turing’s work did not stop at formal theory. During the Second World War, he worked at Britain’s Bletchley Park on German military ciphers. The effort drew on earlier breakthroughs by Polish mathematicians Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki, and Henryk Zygalski, whose methods and intelligence were passed to British and French allies before the war. Turing, Gordon Welchman, Harold Keen, and a much larger engineering and operations workforce helped design and run the Bombe machines that searched for Enigma settings. The Bombe was electromechanical, specialized codebreaking equipment; it was not a stored-program general-purpose computer. Keeping those categories distinct makes the achievement more impressive, not less: mathematics, electromechanical design, captured material, expert operators, and careful intelligence work had to fit together.

In 1945 Turing wrote a design for the Automatic Computing Engine, or ACE, a stored-program electronic computer that was strikingly ambitious for its time. In “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” (1950), he asked how we might judge whether a machine could convincingly take part in a text conversation. His “imitation game” turned an expansive philosophical question into an experimental one. It did not establish that fluent conversation equals human understanding; it gave later artificial-intelligence researchers a testable framing and a rich debate that is still alive.

Turing was prosecuted in 1952 for homosexuality under the laws of his time and was subjected to state-enforced hormonal treatment. He died in 1954. His foundational work therefore sits alongside a stark reminder that institutions can benefit from a scientist’s labor while persecuting the person who performed it.

## 3. From logic to electronic computers

Turing made computation mathematically legible. Turning the same idea into a machine required several parallel inventions. In 1937, Shannon connected Boolean algebra with telephone relays. George Stibitz built a relay-based binary adder at Bell Labs. In Germany, Konrad Zuse built a series of programmable machines, culminating in the relay-based Z3 in 1941, and devised an early high-level programming notation called Plankalkül. In the United States, John Atanasoff and Clifford Berry built an electronic, binary machine for solving systems of linear equations; it was specialized rather than a general-purpose stored-program computer. Each project answered a different “first” depending on whether one means electronic, binary, programmable, automatic, or general-purpose.

During the war, the British team led by Tommy Flowers built Colossus, an electronic machine used to analyze Lorenz cipher traffic. It was configurable for that task and enormously consequential, yet remained secret for decades. In 1945 and 1946, J. Presper Eckert and John Mauchly’s team at the University of Pennsylvania’s Moore School completed ENIAC. Built for the U.S. Army’s Ballistic Research Laboratory, it used thousands of vacuum tubes and could perform programmed calculations far faster than the electromechanical machines before it. When it was publicly demonstrated in 1946, “programming” meant a physical redesign of switch settings, plugs, and cables. The machine’s speed was electronic; changing its job still took careful human work.

![Jean Jennings Bartik and Frances Bilas Spence prepare ENIAC for its 1946 demonstration. U.S. Army photo.](/images/computer-science-history/eniac-programmers.jpg)

The women who first programmed ENIAC were Jean Jennings Bartik, Betty Snyder Holberton, Kathleen McNulty, Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer, Frances Bilas Spence, and Ruth Lichterman Teitelbaum. Recruited from the Army’s human-computing staff, they learned the machine from circuit diagrams, invented programming techniques, and translated mathematical work into reliable configurations. Because the machine was initially programmed by switches and cables, much of this expertise was treated as operating work rather than recognized as software design. It was software design. Meanwhile, Eckert, Mauchly, John von Neumann, Herman Goldstine, Arthur Burks, and others argued over how to make programs easier to load and reuse. Von Neumann’s widely circulated 1945 EDVAC report helped popularize the stored-program architecture, but that architecture grew from collaborative work and ideas already being explored on both sides of the Atlantic.

By 1948, the Manchester Baby had run a stored program in electronic memory; Maurice Wilkes and colleagues brought the EDSAC into regular service at Cambridge in 1949. Turing’s ACE design, the EDVAC project, the Baby, and EDSAC differed, but they converged on a key shift: keep instructions in the same fast memory as data, then fetch, interpret, and execute them. It became possible to change a computer’s purpose chiefly by changing the program rather than rewiring its entire logic.

The new machines depended on work done by people outside the “computer designer” role, too: women who calculated trajectories by hand, technicians who built and repaired circuits, machinists who made tolerances real, operators who found faults, and mathematicians who devised the numerical methods that made a result useful. The Smithsonian has also documented the Black women mathematicians who computed for NACA and NASA. Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan, and Mary Jackson built careers in human computation and then worked through the transition to electronic machines. The hardware story is incomplete without the labor that taught it what to calculate.

## 4. Programming becomes a field of its own

Once computers could retain a program, the next challenge was how people should describe one. Early programmers wrote machine code or assembly language: short symbolic names for operations that still corresponded closely to a processor’s circuitry. That approach could be precise and efficient, but it made large programs expensive to write, move, explain, and maintain.

Grace Murray Hopper helped make programming more abstract. After work on the Harvard Mark I, she joined the Eckert–Mauchly Computer Corporation and led efforts toward reusable translation software. Her A-0 system, produced in the early 1950s, helped turn program fragments into executable machine instructions; historians debate whether it fits every strict definition of a compiler, but it was an important step toward the idea. Hopper also championed machine-independent business languages. FLOW-MATIC influenced the design of COBOL, a language shaped by a committee and adopted widely for commercial data processing.

![U.S. Navy Captain Grace Hopper receives her promotion certificate in an official service photograph.](/images/computer-science-history/grace-hopper.jpg)

Image: U.S. Navy photograph, public domain in the United States; [Wikimedia Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:428-GX-USN_1157762_(25700694682).jpg).

In 1957, John Backus and an IBM team released FORTRAN, designed to make scientific and engineering calculations easier to express and compile efficiently. John McCarthy created Lisp in 1958, which became central to symbolic artificial intelligence and remains influential in language design. The international committee behind ALGOL helped develop a notation and structured approach that shaped later languages. Over the following decades, languages such as Pascal, C, Smalltalk, SQL, Java, Python, and many others changed how programmers represent problems and work together. Each one carries design tradeoffs: how much the language checks, what it makes easy to express, what hardware it runs on, and how its community shares libraries and conventions.

The machine’s operating system became just as consequential as the programming language. Early systems ran one job at a time, with human operators managing job queues. Time-sharing systems such as MIT’s CTSS let multiple people use one computer through terminals. Multics explored a powerful, secure, hierarchical operating environment. Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie at Bell Labs distilled some of those lessons into Unix, then helped make Unix portable by creating the C language. Universities, firms, and users adapted Unix widely. The pattern that a stable operating system can support many programs—and that shared, documented interfaces let independent teams build on each other—underlies much of today’s software.

Other researchers made software more dependable and its data more useful. Edsger Dijkstra developed influential methods for reasoning about structured programs and graph algorithms. Tony Hoare formalized program correctness. Donald Knuth’s *The Art of Computer Programming* gave algorithms rigorous treatment. Edgar F. Codd’s relational database model (1970) made tables, relations, and declarative queries a durable way to organize data. Richard Hamming developed error-correcting codes; Whitfield Diffie, Martin Hellman, Ronald Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman helped establish modern public-key cryptography. Distributed-systems researchers Leslie Lamport, Barbara Liskov, and many others showed how much harder it is to keep computers in agreement when messages are delayed, machines fail, or participants cannot be trusted. This is what the discipline became: not simply “how to code,” but the study of algorithms, information, languages, systems, security, interaction, and proof.

## 5. The silicon revolution puts a computer within reach

Vacuum tubes made electronic switching possible but consumed considerable power, generated heat, and took up space. In 1947 at Bell Labs, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain demonstrated the point-contact transistor while working in a group led by William Shockley. A transistor could act as a switch or amplifier in a much smaller, lower-power package. The invention was not one simple handoff: Shockley, Bardeen, Brattain, and the Bell Labs research organization made different and sometimes contentious contributions to the device family.

Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments and Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor developed integrated-circuit approaches in 1958–59. Rather than wire every component separately, an integrated circuit could put interconnected electronic parts on a single piece of semiconductor. Jean Hoerni’s planar process, the work of Fairchild’s “traitorous eight,” and the rise of reliable silicon manufacturing were essential to making this approach scalable. Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng’s MOS transistor work provided a switch that could be made densely and economically. Gordon Moore’s 1965 observation about rising component density became a forecast and then an industry target, although it was never a law of nature.

![An original explanatory illustration of the path from transistor to integrated circuit to microprocessor.](/images/computer-science-history/semiconductor.svg)

In 1971, Intel’s 4004 put a central-processing function on one chip. Its creation depended on Federico Faggin’s silicon-gate implementation, Ted Hoff’s architectural ideas, Stan Mazor’s design contributions, and Masatoshi Shima’s detailed logic design. As processing, memory, and storage became cheaper, a computer no longer had to belong to a government lab, a university, or a large corporation. The 1975 MITS Altair 8800 helped spark the hobbyist computer movement. Members of the Homebrew Computer Club shared ideas and prototypes. Steve Wozniak’s Apple I and Apple II designs and the IBM PC (1981) helped broaden personal computing; the Macintosh (1984) popularized a mouse-driven graphical interface.

The route to a friendly computer screen was longer than a single company’s product launch. Douglas Engelbart’s 1968 “Mother of All Demos” showed a mouse, hypertext links, videoconferencing, and collaborative editing. Researchers at Xerox PARC, including Alan Kay, Adele Goldberg, and others, developed the Alto and explored overlapping windows, networking, and personal workstations. These ideas moved through institutions, prototypes, and commercial products before they became common. A graphical desktop was a computer-science result, a design practice, and a social agreement about what a computer should feel like.

## 6. ARPANET: a beginning, not the whole Internet

In the early 1960s, J. C. R. Licklider at the U.S. Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) described an “Intergalactic Computer Network” in which people and computers could access programs and information remotely. The technical problem was that ordinary telephone networks expected one continuous circuit to be held open for a conversation. Computer data, by contrast, arrived in uneven bursts. Could messages be split into packets, routed independently, and reassembled at their destination?

Paul Baran at RAND in the United States and Donald Davies at Britain’s National Physical Laboratory independently developed packet-network ideas. Davies coined the word “packet.” Leonard Kleinrock’s mathematical work on queueing and packet networks contributed a theoretical foundation. ARPA director Bob Taylor pushed for one network to connect research computers; Larry Roberts led the network design and procurement. Bolt Beranek and Newman (BBN), with Frank Heart and a team of engineers, built the Interface Message Processors that connected the first host computers. The project drew on work across agencies and countries. The ARPANET was a research network intended to connect computing resources and research groups; it was not created from a single “survive nuclear war” blueprint, a simplified explanation that often obscures its actual design history.

![A 1969 DARPA sketch of the first four ARPANET nodes, drawn on a napkin.](/images/computer-science-history/arpanet-napkin.jpg)

Image: DARPA, May 1969; public-domain federal image, reproduced through [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Map_of_the_4_node_arpanet,_of_1969,_drawn_on_a_napkin.jpg).

On 29 October 1969, UCLA student programmer Charley Kline tried to log in to the computer at the Stanford Research Institute (SRI). The first two letters, “L” and “O,” arrived before the receiving machine crashed. After the engineers restarted it, the full word LOGIN was sent. The first network message was an accidental “LO,” a small and human start for a system that would eventually connect billions of people. UCLA, SRI, the University of California at Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah formed the initial four nodes.

The first nodes did not yet create the Internet as we use the term. They formed one network. The bigger breakthrough was learning how separate networks—with different hardware and local rules—could interconnect. Bob Kahn and Vint Cerf developed the end-to-end internetworking approach that became TCP/IP. In broad terms, IP gives a packet an address and a route across networks; TCP helps two programs establish a reliable, ordered stream. The ARPANET switched from its earlier NCP protocol to TCP/IP on 1 January 1983, a coordinated changeover often called a flag day. That date is a useful marker for the modern Internet, but it represents years of research, testing, and implementation.

The Internet grew through contributions that are easy to miss if its history is told as a list of U.S. government projects. Ray Tomlinson adapted network email and chose the @ symbol to separate a user from a host. Steve Crocker and the Network Working Group used Requests for Comments (RFCs) as a collaborative way to develop protocols. Jon Postel coordinated names and numbers for decades. Paul Mockapetris designed the Domain Name System. Bob Metcalfe and his colleagues developed Ethernet for local networks. Radia Perlman’s work on spanning-tree bridges helped make large switched networks practical. Academic networks such as NSFNET and Britain’s JANET helped expand access; commercial providers and international operators carried the infrastructure onward.

There is also a history of how the Internet reached communities beyond its early U.S. research hubs. In Ghana, computer scientist Nii Quaynor established one of West Africa’s first Internet service providers and helped build African network institutions. Pioneers throughout Africa, Asia, Latin America, Europe, and the Middle East built local links, peering points, registries, policy, and expertise. An Internet connection is not just a protocol: it depends on cables, radio links, electricity, affordable devices, national policy, and people who know how to operate it.

## 7. A German intrusion and the birth of a public cybersecurity story

Network connectivity made collaboration easier. It also meant that an account on one computer could become a path to other machines, and early networks were not designed with today’s security assumptions. Two German episodes are often blended together, so it helps to keep them separate.

In 1984, members of the Hamburg-based Chaos Computer Club publicly demonstrated a vulnerability in Bildschirmtext (BTX), a German videotex service run by the postal system. Steffen Wernéry and Wau Holland showed that the system’s billing design could be abused to trigger repeated charges. They returned the money they extracted and used the demonstration to force attention onto the flaw. It was a deliberately public security test and political intervention, not the first computer intrusion in history.

![An original timeline of the Cuckoo’s Egg investigation: a billing anomaly, a cross-network trace, and public disclosure.](/images/computer-science-history/cyber-case.svg)

From 1986 into 1987, a different group of West German hackers broke into computers in the United States and elsewhere and passed information to Soviet intelligence. This became known as the KGB hack. The story is often described as the first documented international case of cyber-espionage, but it is not a reliable claim for the first “hack” of any kind. Nor were the participants simply a group of students at a university; they came from a particular hacker milieu, and popular retellings sometimes flatten their different backgrounds into one label.

At Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, astronomer and systems administrator Clifford Stoll was asked to investigate a 75-cent accounting discrepancy. Instead of dismissing a tiny balance, he treated it as evidence. He compared accounting logs, watched activity, kept a detailed paper trail, and worked with telephone companies and law-enforcement officials to trace the connection beyond his lab. His 1988 article, “Stalking the Wily Hacker,” became one of the earliest detailed public accounts of a network-intrusion investigation; his 1989 book *The Cuckoo’s Egg* carried the story to a much wider audience. Stoll showed that patient measurement and incident records could turn a barely visible anomaly into an investigation spanning organizations and national borders.

The case helped give the public a vocabulary for network security: unauthorized access, monitoring, incident response, evidence, attribution, jurisdiction, and responsible disclosure. It also showed how weak assumptions in one system could be chained into access elsewhere. From that point forward, computer science history includes security as a core systems question. Authentication, cryptography, network design, software updates, forensic process, and human judgment all affect whether a connected computer remains trustworthy.

## 8. The World Wide Web makes the Internet navigable

The Internet and the Web are related, but they are not the same thing. The Internet is the global network of networks and the protocols that move data among them. The Web is one service that uses that infrastructure: a system of linked documents and other resources, addressed by URLs and transferred with HTTP, commonly displayed as HTML pages.

At CERN, physicist and computer scientist Tim Berners-Lee was trying to help a large, international research community keep track of information spread across many systems. His March 1989 proposal combined hypertext with network access. In 1990 he and Belgian systems engineer Robert Cailliau formalized the project. Berners-Lee implemented the first browser/editor and web server on a NeXT workstation. The first website, at `info.cern.ch`, explained the Web project and how to build a server. In August 1991, Berners-Lee announced the software on Internet newsgroups; on 30 April 1993 CERN made it available without royalties, enabling others to implement and extend it.

The first Web browser was capable but ran on a specialized NeXT platform. Nicola Pellow wrote a line-mode browser that worked on many kinds of computers. In 1993, the NCSA Mosaic browser made images and links feel approachable on ordinary machines. Marc Andreessen and Eric Bina were among its developers. Mosaic was followed by commercial browsers such as Netscape Navigator; web standards and server software spread as businesses, universities, publishers, and ordinary users found reasons to be online. The Web did not replace email, file transfer, or other Internet services. It added an especially flexible way to publish and connect information.

Search engines made pages discoverable at larger scale. E-commerce made the Web a marketplace. Blogs, wikis, photo sharing, and social networks made publishing and collaboration part of everyday use. Smartphones placed Web-connected sensors, cameras, maps, and messaging in people’s pockets. Those products depended on many fields at once: networking, distributed storage, databases, interface design, compression, cryptography, and the work of standards groups that helped independent implementations communicate.

## 9. Cloud computing: computers as a service

The cloud can sound like a new kind of computer floating somewhere above the network. It is more grounded than that. A cloud provider operates data centers full of ordinary and specialized computers, storage systems, network equipment, backup power, cooling systems, and software. The provider bundles these resources behind network APIs so a customer can request capacity without buying, installing, and maintaining every physical machine.

This idea has older ancestors. Mainframe time-sharing let many users share an expensive computer. Virtual machines separated a software-defined computer from the particular physical hardware underneath it. Utility-computing proposals imagined computing as something customers could draw on when needed. What changed in the 2000s was the scale of commodity servers and global networks, combined with automation that could provision virtual machines and storage through software interfaces.

![An original diagram showing how applications reach shared compute and storage running in distributed data centers.](/images/computer-science-history/cloud-stack.svg)

Amazon Web Services introduced S3 storage in 2006 and EC2 compute later that year. Google App Engine and Microsoft Azure followed with their own platform and infrastructure services. Cloud offerings span infrastructure as a service, managed platforms and databases, and finished applications delivered through a browser. Containers and orchestration systems later made it easier to package and schedule services across large server fleets. The business model changes who buys the hardware; the engineering still depends on operating systems, networking, distributed algorithms, observability, and security.

Cloud platforms made experiments faster and global services easier to scale. They also concentrate infrastructure in a small number of providers, creating questions about outages, vendor dependence, data jurisdiction, privacy, cyber risk, energy use, and the cost of moving away. The cloud is a major computing shift, not an escape from physical constraints: processors, cables, energy, water for cooling, and skilled human operators remain part of the system.

## 10. Artificial intelligence: from theorem proving to generative models

The modern AI story begins before computers were powerful enough to make today’s systems possible. In 1943, neuroscientist Warren McCulloch and mathematician Walter Pitts published a mathematical model of neurons as logical units. Donald Hebb proposed a theory of learning through changing connections between neurons. Turing’s 1950 paper asked how to evaluate machine conversation. In 1956, John McCarthy organized the Dartmouth Summer Research Project with Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon. Their proposal coined the name *artificial intelligence* and offered a bold conjecture: aspects of learning and intelligence might be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate them.

Early symbolic AI tried to represent facts and rules explicitly. Allen Newell, Herbert A. Simon, and Cliff Shaw built the Logic Theorist and later the General Problem Solver. McCarthy created Lisp, which made recursive symbolic programs easier to express. Frank Rosenblatt’s perceptron explored a different path: a simple network that could adjust its weights from examples. Early promises ran into limits in data, computing power, and the systems’ ability to handle cases beyond the carefully prepared demonstrations. Funding and expectations rose and fell through successive periods sometimes called AI winters.

Neural networks did not disappear. Paul Werbos described backpropagation in an early form; David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams helped popularize it in 1986 as a practical way to train multilayer networks. Yann LeCun showed how convolutional networks could learn visual features useful for reading handwritten digits. Judea Pearl developed influential probabilistic reasoning methods. Richard Sutton and Andrew Barto shaped reinforcement learning. Each line of work had different goals, and each depended on mathematical theory, data, hardware, and research communities that extended across decades.

![An original high-level illustration of tokenization, attention, model layers, and next-token prediction.](/images/computer-science-history/transformer.svg)

Modern deep learning accelerated when the pieces aligned: large labelled datasets, graphics processors capable of parallel numerical work, improved optimization methods, and neural architectures that could use more examples effectively. In 2012, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton’s AlexNet sharply improved image classification on the ImageNet competition. In 2017, Ashish Vaswani and seven colleagues introduced the Transformer, an architecture built around attention mechanisms and designed to process sequences in parallel. Researchers and companies then trained large language models on vast collections of text and code, often adapting them to follow instructions through further training and human feedback.

Generative AI systems can produce text, code, images, audio, and video because they learn statistical structure from examples and generate likely continuations conditioned on an input. Large-scale systems combine model architecture with training data, high-performance accelerators, distributed computing, cloud data centers, evaluation, and deployment interfaces. The public release of conversational assistants in the early 2020s made this work visible to people who had never used a machine-learning library. By 2026, generative systems have become common software tools and research instruments, while new model capabilities and costs continue to shift quickly.

“Generative” does not mean “infallible,” and a fluent answer is not proof that a system has verified its claims. Models can reproduce bias in data, invent convincing details, expose sensitive information, and be difficult to interpret. Training data can raise questions about consent and intellectual property. Inference and training consume compute and energy. People also disagree about how to measure advanced capabilities, distribute benefits, govern powerful systems, and keep humans accountable for high-impact decisions. These are not side issues outside computer science: they touch data management, security, evaluation, interface design, ethics, economics, and the properties of learning algorithms themselves.

## The deeper pattern

Across two centuries, computing has grown through a repeating cycle. Someone makes an abstract idea precise. Someone else finds a way to build it from available materials. Teams turn a prototype into a dependable tool. Communities invent conventions so that tools can work together. New uses reveal new weaknesses and new possibilities. Then another generation changes the abstraction: gears become relays, relays become transistors, a wired room becomes a program, isolated computers become an Internet, pages become a Web, and fixed rules give way to systems trained from data.

Turing’s machine remains at the center of this story because it gave computer science a clear language for talking about computation itself. But the working computer also required Lovelace’s insistence that machines could manipulate symbols, Boole’s logic, Shannon’s circuit model, the builders and programmers of the 1940s, the semiconductor industry, operating-system and language designers, network engineers, Web standards, cloud operators, and the researchers who continue to test the claims made for AI.

The field is therefore not a finished monument to a handful of celebrated inventors. It is a shared human project. Its history is an invitation to ask better questions about who builds systems, whose labor gets acknowledged, who can use them, whose data powers them, how they fail, and what we choose to make possible next.
